Medieval fortification, 11th to 15th century

Anatomy of a Castle

Every part of a castle did one job: make the attacker's next step harder than the last. Pick a part below to see what it was for.

Ditch or moat

A ditch cut around the outer wall, wet or dry. It holds siege towers and rams back from the wall, makes ladders longer and harder to handle, and stops miners tunnelling under the wall, because a tunnel beneath water floods. Many castles had a dry ditch only. A moat full of water needed a river or spring to feed it.

Stops: siege towers, battering rams, mining

What attackers tried, and the answer

Medieval armies had a small set of tools. Castle builders learned an answer to each.

Scaling ladders

Tall walls kept the climb long, and a ditch kept ladder bases far from the foot of the wall. Defenders on the wall-walk could push ladders off or drop stones on the climbers.

Siege towers

A wide ditch stopped a heavy tower from rolling up to the wall. Towers covered in fresh animal hides were harder to set alight, so defenders shot fire arrows and used water.

Battering rams

The ditch, a raised drawbridge, a portcullis and barred doors all stood between a ram and the gate. Overhanging parapets let defenders drop heavy objects onto the ram's crew.

Mining under the wall

Water-filled ditches, foundations cut into rock, round towers with no corners to undermine, and counter-mines dug by defenders to meet the attackers' tunnel.

Trebuchets and catapults

Thick stone walls with a sloping base. Defenders often had engines of their own on the towers to fire back at the crews.

Starvation

Wells inside the walls, stores of grain, and livestock kept in the bailey. At Kenilworth in 1266 the garrison held out for about six months.

Getting through the gate

The gate was the weakest point of any wall, so it received the most defences. A well-built gatehouse made an attacker pass through all of these.

  1. DrawbridgeRaised at night or under threat, leaving a gap across the ditch.
  2. BarbicanA walled forecourt in front of the gate. Attackers bunched up inside it while archers fired from above.
  3. PortcullisA heavy grille of timber or iron-shod wood, dropped down grooves in the gate passage.
  4. Murder holesOpenings in the ceiling of the passage. Defenders dropped stones through them or poured water to put out fires.
  5. Barred doorsThick timber doors held shut by a heavy beam across the back.
  6. Second portcullisDropped behind anyone who got through, trapping them in the passage under fire.

Bent entrances. Some castles made the path through the gate turn sharply. That slowed a charge, gave defenders a clear shot at an attacker's unshielded right side, and kept a ram from getting a straight run.

Details that mattered

Arrow loops

Narrow slits, wide on the inside. An archer had a broad angle to shoot across, while an enemy outside had a tiny target. Crossbow loops often added a horizontal slot for aiming.

Sloping wall bases

A plinth or batter thickened the foot of the wall. Stones dropped from above bounced outward into attackers, and the extra mass made undermining harder.

Hoardings and machicolations

Timber galleries (hoardings), later replaced by stone ones (machicolations), jutted from the top of the wall. Defenders could drop things straight down onto anyone at the base.

A myth to be careful with

People often say spiral stairs turned clockwise going up so right-handed defenders had room to swing a sword. Historians dispute it, and many castle stairs turn the other way.

Why castles stopped working

No castle was unbeatable. Most fell to hunger, treachery or a long siege rather than a single assault. From the 15th century, powerful cannon could crack tall stone walls, and fortresses were rebuilt lower and thicker, with angled earth-backed bastions that absorbed shot.

Terms

Bailey
The open yard inside the walls, with stables, workshops and storerooms.
Barbican
A fortified outwork guarding the approach to a gate.
Bartizan
A small turret hanging from the corner of a wall or tower.
Crenel and merlon
The gap and the solid block alternating along a parapet.
Curtain wall
The main wall enclosing the castle's courtyard.
Embrasure
An opening in a wall, usually splayed inside, for shooting through.
Motte
An artificial earth mound that carried an early timber tower.
Portcullis
A sliding grille that closes a gate passage.
Postern
A small back door, used for sallies or slipping messengers out.
Wall-walk
The walkway along the top of a wall, behind the parapet.
Sizes and dates are typical, not universal. Castles varied widely by region, period and budget.

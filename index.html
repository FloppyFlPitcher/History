How castle defences developed

Castles changed as attackers did. Each stage below answers a weakness in the one before it.

  1. 9th and 10th centuriesThe first castles

    European castles appeared after the Carolingian Empire broke up and local lords took power. In 864, Charles the Bald banned building castles without his permission. The oldest castle still standing in Europe, at Doué-la-Fontaine in France, was converted into a keep around 950. [9]

  2. 11th centuryMotte and bailey

    A timber tower on an earth mound, 4.5 to 9 metres high, beside a courtyard with a wooden palisade and a ditch. It was common in France and in Norman Britain. No skilled masons were needed: one average mound is estimated to have taken 50 people about 40 days. The weakness was timber, which burned and rotted. [14][9]

  3. 12th centuryStone keeps

    Wealthier builders switched to stone, which lasted longer but cost far more and took longer to build. Some mottes received a stone shell keep on top, and by the 12th century the main tower was often stone. Dover Castle cost about £7,000 between 1181 and 1191. [14][9]

  4. From the 1180sRound towers

    Towers changed from square to D-shaped, then round, giving archers a greater range of fire with no corner blind spots. [2]

  5. 1196 to 1198Château Gaillard

    Richard the Lionheart built it in about two years, at a reported cost of around £12,000. It had three baileys split by rock-cut ditches and was one of the first European castles with machicolations. [12]

  6. 13th centuryConcentric castles

    Caerphilly, begun in 1268, was the first concentric castle in Britain. Beaumaris followed in 1295, and Krak des Chevaliers gained its outer ring in the early 13th century. [11][4][10]

  7. 14th and 15th centuriesGunpowder

    Gunpowder reached Europe in the 1320s, but it changed castle building only in the 15th century, when cannon could breach stone walls. By the 1450s guns were the preferred siege weapon. [9]

Ditch or moat

A ditch around the outer wall, permanently or temporarily filled with water. It was deep enough to slow horses, foot soldiers and siege towers, and attackers had to fill or bridge it before they could bring a tower or ram up to the wall. Even half a metre of water was enough to hinder them while defenders shot from above.

What attackers tried, and the answer

Medieval armies had a small set of tools. Castle builders learned an answer to each.

Scaling ladders

Tall walls kept the climb long, and a moat or trench had to be dealt with before ladders or towers could be brought close. [2][3]

Siege towers

Defenders shot fire arrows at them, so attackers covered towers in water-soaked animal skins or metal plates. The moat had to be filled first, and loose earth in a trench could make a tower collapse. [3]

Battering rams

Defenders dangled chains, ropes and hooks to overturn rams and dropped heavy objects from above. A drawbridge, portcullis and heavy doors also stood between a ram and the gate. [2][3]

Mining under the wall

Mining did not work on castles built on solid rock, and a stone sloping base made undermining harder. Defenders dug counter-mines to meet the tunnel, then used fire or smoke to drive the miners out or collapsed it. [3]

Trebuchets and catapults

Thick stone walls, and strong towers that could carry trebuchets of their own to shoot back at the besiegers. [4]

Starvation

Stored food, and sometimes secret tunnels to bring supplies in. Edward I's Welsh castles were sited by the sea so they could be resupplied by water. At Kenilworth in 1266 the garrison held out for 172 days before hunger and dysentery forced them out. [1][3]

Getting through the gate

The gate was the weakest point of any wall, so it received the most defences. A well-built gatehouse made an attacker pass through all of these.

  1. DrawbridgeRaised on chains, or swung aside, leaving a gap across the ditch. [2]
  2. BarbicanA fortified outwork protecting the gate. Some were walled courtyards in front of it, covered by fire from nearby towers. [2]
  3. PortcullisA heavy wooden or iron grille, lowered to block the entrance. [2][8]
  4. Murder holesOpenings in the ceiling of the passage. Defenders dropped missiles or hot liquids on anyone who had got past the outer door. Boiling oil was usually too expensive, so boiling water and quicklime were more common. [5]
  5. Heavy doorA thick wooden door, the last barrier at ground level. [2]
  6. Second portcullisMany gates had one or two portcullises, so anyone who got through the first could still be stuck in the passage. [2]

Bent approaches. By the mid-13th century, barbicans were set further out from the gate and often included a 90-degree turn to slow anyone approaching it. [2]

Details that mattered

Arrow loops

Narrow openings in the wall with tapered sides, which gave the archer a wider view. Some stood up to nine feet tall. [5]

Sloping wall bases

A sloping plinth at the foot of the wall made undermining and scaling harder. Objects dropped from above bounced off it at unpredictable angles into the attackers. [2][3]

Hoardings and machicolations

Machicolations were openings hanging off the side of a wall. Defenders used them to shoot, drop rocks or pour hot substances on attackers still trying to get through. Wooden hoardings served archers on the walls. [3][5]

A myth to be careful with

People often say spiral stairs turned clockwise going up so right-handed defenders had room to swing a sword. There is no primary source evidence for it, and anticlockwise stairs exist at Goodrich, Kenilworth and Norwich castles. The idea seems to trace back to a 1902 book. [6]

Why castles stopped working

No castle was unbeatable. Kenilworth fell to hunger and disease, and a corner of Rochester's keep fell to miners. Cannon from the 14th century, widespread by the 15th, fired iron shot that damaged masonry more than stone shot did. Thickening walls was slow and costly, so many nobles moved into comfortable manor houses, and castles became more about display. [7]

Terms

Bailey
The open yard inside the walls, with stables, workshops and storerooms.
Barbican
A fortified outwork guarding the approach to a gate.
Bartizan
A small turret hanging from the corner of a wall or tower.
Crenel and merlon
The gap and the solid block alternating along a parapet.
Curtain wall
The main wall enclosing the castle's courtyard.
Embrasure
An opening in a wall, usually splayed inside, for shooting through.
Motte
An artificial earth mound that carried an early timber tower.
Portcullis
A sliding grille that closes a gate passage.
Postern
A small back door, used for sallies or slipping messengers out.
Wall-walk
The walkway along the top of a wall, behind the parapet.
